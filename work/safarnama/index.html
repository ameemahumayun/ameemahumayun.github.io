Team Project · UX Research + Product Design

Designing trust into domestic tourism.

Safarnama ("people, not places") is a tourism app connecting travelers directly with local hosts, built with a five-person university team from survey and interview research through to a tested Figma prototype.

01 — Overview

The disconnect in domestic tourism.

Tourism tends to favor the traveler's experience, keeping visitors inside pre-packaged tours and commercialized attractions with little room for meaningful local engagement. Locals, meanwhile, are rich in cultural knowledge and skills but often lack structured, financially viable ways to share them — and the digital spaces that exist aren't designed to actually improve the tourist–local relationship, while informal, non-digital arrangements stay inconsistent and constrained by poor infrastructure.

Safarnama is our team's answer to that gap: an app where travelers and locals connect directly, as "Travel Buddies" and "Tour Guides," rather than through a packaged, anonymized layer in between.

02 — My role

Across the project, with product design as the focus.

I was involved across every part of this project — research design, fieldwork, synthesis, and testing — and predominantly led the product and UI design, including the Figma prototype shown throughout this case study. The work was done with a five-person team: Muhammad Ahmad Tehseen, Sheikh Shahzaib Ali, Sana Humayun, and Arqam Sajjad.

03 — Research process

Quant to size the problem, qual to understand it.

Research ran in two stages: a broad quantitative survey to size out where travelers and locals were actually falling short of each other, then qualitative interviews to understand why — followed by usability testing once a prototype existed to test against.

04 — Key findings

What the numbers and the interviews agreed on.

01

What travelers actually want

Combined culture, food, thrill, fun, and social experiences were the most popular draw (15.6%), ahead of fun alone (9.1%) or social experiences alone (5.2%). For finding it, social media dominates — alone or paired with travel blogs (15.6% each), or with Google (11.7%) — while locals as an exclusive information source trailed at 7.8%, on par with travel blogs alone.

02

Willingness outpaces actual connection

About 49% of travelers said they were willing to engage with locals, against 31% negative and 20% conditional or hesitant. But when asked about their actual experience with local help, 56.2% reported neutral or no significant interaction, versus 43.8% positive — willingness is there; the connection mostly isn't happening. Roughly a quarter reported no significant issues at all; where issues came up, they were network problems, poor sanitation, and misguidance through Google Maps.

03

Locals are ready; the systems aren't

Most tourist–local contact still begins through businesses — shops, transport, guesthouses — and locals consistently named strong community networks as essential to sustainable tourism. There's real economic appetite (homestays, food stalls, tour services) alongside real concern about non-local, capitalistic organizations moving in to monopolize growing tourist markets.

04

Infrastructure, trust, and culture are the same problem

Poor internet, low literacy, cash-only systems, and word-of-mouth coordination hinder structure — yet locals are already heavy users of TikTok, Instagram, and YouTube, and prefer audio or video over text. Trust is fragile in both directions: travelers report price hikes and unclear expectations, locals report distrust and disputed payments, and the absence of a structured system is what lets both happen. Underneath it, locals raised real cultural-tension concerns — overlooked norms, non-consensual photography, and fear of cultural deterioration similar to Murree as areas get overrun.

05 — From findings to features

Three principles, four features.

The findings translated into three product principles: let people sign up as different roles, give each role different privileges, and let travelers and locals connect with each other directly rather than through an intermediary.

Sign-up flow

Sign-up runs on phone number and a photo of a CNIC, which the app parses to auto-fill information, with OTP for sign-in — designed around uneven literacy while keeping the account verified and secure.

Roles

Users sign up as a traveler, a host, or both — registering as a "Travel Buddy" or a "Tour Guide" respectively, with different privileges per role, and can switch roles from the settings menu at any time.

Explore

Travelers browse spots, activities, and food with reviews for each; a crowdsourced map surfaces local information, and reviews submitted by locals get precedence on it.

Everything else

An SOS call feature, downloadable emergency-protocol PDFs, and an open "Other Services" listing where locals can freelance whatever service they want to offer.

06 — Testing & results

Where the prototype held up, and where it didn't.

26 participants ran task-based usability tests across both the traveler and host flows. Task times varied widely — switching from a traveler to a local view took 6 seconds on average, while using the map to find a location and read its reviews took closest to 48 seconds, with signing up and logging in as a traveler close behind at 37 seconds.

How testers felt, in their own words

The dominant reactions were confident, satisfied, and comfortable. A smaller but real minority reported feeling hesitant, confused, or frustrated — mostly tied to specific navigation and terminology issues rather than the concept itself.

01

Labels didn't always match mental models

"Explore" was sometimes misread as the home screen, and the split between profile and settings confused users who were following logic the prototype didn't actually support — a reminder that icons, labels, and layout all have to agree with what users already expect.

02

Terminology needed more scaffolding

"Local" and "Host," or "Traveller" and "Guest," weren't interchangeable for users the way we'd assumed. A one-line explanation usually resolved the confusion on the spot — evidence that a clear onboarding tutorial and help screen are necessary, not optional polish.

03

Reception was genuinely split on some calls

Playful terminology like "Buddy Up" drew opposing reactions, and visual consistency was praised by some testers and criticized by others — both flagged as needing further, more targeted testing rather than a single fix.

What worked without qualification: the SOS feature was universally praised. Testers found it practical despite other prototype flaws, several said they'd use the app in real life, and the consistency between the traveler and host profiles was specifically called out as making the app "easier."

07 — Reflection

What this represents.

Safarnama was a university team project, built and tested end to end rather than shipped — the research, the product decisions, and the usability findings above are all real, but the prototype hasn't gone further than that. What I'd take from it: the willingness-to-connect numbers and the trust-and-terminology issues surfaced in testing point at the same thing from two different methods, which is usually a sign the finding is real rather than a survey artifact.
